# Business writing

The goal is a document a busy person understands on the first read and can act on.

## Before writing

Answer three questions, then write.

1. Who reads this, and what do they already know?
2. What should they do or decide after reading it?
3. What kind of document is it? Pick one type below and keep to its shape.

## Put the point first

- The first lines carry the conclusion, the request or the decision needed. Background comes after, and only as much as the reader needs.
- Every paragraph opens with its main sentence. A reader who reads only the first sentence of each paragraph should still get the argument.
- Headings say the point, not the topic. "Costs fall 18% in year two", not "Cost analysis".

## Shapes for common documents

- **Email.** A subject line that says what the email is for. One opening sentence with the request or the news. Short paragraphs for the details. A last line with the next step and a date. A plain sign-off. No greeting filler.
- **Proposal.** The problem in the client's terms. What you will do. What they get and when. The price and what it covers. What you need from them. The next step.
- **Report.** A summary of findings and the recommendation at the top. Then the evidence, one finding per section. Then method and limits. Numbers go in tables.
- **Meeting notes.** Date and attendees. Decisions made. Action items in a table with owner and due date. Open questions. Leave out who said what unless it matters.
- **Procedure or how-to.** What the procedure achieves and what you need before starting. Numbered steps, one action per step, each written as a command. The expected result after the steps that have one. Put a warning before the step it guards, never after.
- **Memo or one-pager.** The decision needed. The recommendation. Two or three options with cost and risk in a table. Why this recommendation.
- **Cover letter.** The role and why you fit it, in the first two sentences. Two or three specific things you did, with results. One sentence on why this company. A short close.
- **Plan.** The goal and the deadline. Milestones with dates in a table. Who owns what. Risks and what you will do about each.
- **Technical document.** One kind per document: a tutorial teaches by doing, a how-to solves one task, a reference lists facts, an explanation gives the reasons. Do not mix them.

## Sentences

- Address the reader as "you". Use the present tense. "Will" is only for things that happen later.
- One thought per sentence. Split a sentence that carries two.
- Put the condition before the instruction: "To cancel the order, reply before Friday."
- Put the common case first and the exceptions after.
- Pick one name for each thing and keep it. A document that says "client", "customer" and "account" for one party describes three parties.
- Make every "it", "they" and "this" point at one obvious thing. Repeat the noun when in doubt.
- Keep "only" and "not" next to the word they change.
- Write dates and amounts in full: "14 November 2026", "USD 1,200 per month". Never "soon", "a few" or "ASAP".
- Use numbered lists for steps in order and bullets for everything else. Use a table when comparing options or listing owners and dates.
- Skip idioms, buzzwords and inside jokes. Someone reading in their second language should follow every sentence.

## Facts

- Use only facts the request, the conversation or the attached material gives you.
- Never invent a name, a number, a date, a price, a quote, a statistic or a source.
- When a needed fact is missing, put a placeholder in square brackets where it belongs, such as [client name] or [delivery date], so the reader can fill it in.
- When the material contradicts itself, say so in the document instead of picking a side silently.

## Language and length

- Write in the language the person asked for. If they did not say, use the language of their request.
- Make the document as long as the job needs and no longer. A one-page request gets one page.
